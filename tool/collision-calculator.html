Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between elastic and inelastic collisions?

In an elastic collision, both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved (objects bounce apart cleanly). In a perfectly inelastic collision, momentum is conserved but kinetic energy is not - the objects stick together and move as one, with some energy lost to heat, sound, or deformation.

How do I calculate velocities after an elastic collision?

v1f = ((m1−m2)×v1 + 2×m2×v2) / (m1+m2), and v2f = ((m2−m1)×v2 + 2×m1×v1) / (m1+m2) - these formulas come from solving the conservation of momentum and conservation of kinetic energy equations together.

Why is momentum always conserved, even in inelastic collisions?

Momentum conservation comes directly from Newton's third law (equal and opposite forces during the collision) and doesn't require energy to be conserved - it holds for any collision as long as no external forces act on the system.

What happens to the 'lost' kinetic energy in an inelastic collision?

It converts to other forms of energy - heat from friction and deformation, sound from the impact, and permanent structural deformation of the colliding objects - rather than disappearing, consistent with the overall conservation of energy.
